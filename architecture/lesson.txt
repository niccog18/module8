Imagine you’re taking an open-book exam. You can bring your notes, textbooks, and study guides. When you encounter a question, you don’t try to remember everything from memory, you look up the relevant information in your materials and then write an answer based on what you found.

That’s RAG. Retrieval-Augmented Generation is a technique where an AI model doesn’t answer questions from memory alone, it first retrieves relevant information from a knowledge base, then generates an answer using that retrieved context.

This is the architecture behind most production AI applications today: customer support bots that reference your company’s documentation, legal assistants that search case law, internal tools that answer questions about company policies, coding assistants that reference your codebase. If an AI app needs to answer questions about specific, recent, or private information, it’s almost certainly using RAG.
Why Not Just Use the LLM Alone?

Language models like GPT-4 or Claude have vast knowledge from their training data. So why not just ask them questions directly?

Three critical limitations:

Hallucination. When a model doesn’t know the answer, it often generates a confident-sounding but fabricated response. You learned about this in Module 1. RAG reduces hallucination by giving the model actual source material to reference.

Knowledge cutoff. Models are trained on data up to a certain date. They don’t know about events, product updates, or policy changes after their training cutoff. RAG gives them access to current information.

Private data. Models don’t know about your company’s internal documents, your personal notes, or any information that wasn’t in public training data. RAG lets them work with your specific data without fine-tuning.

RAG solves all three: it provides the model with verified, current, relevant source material, so the model generates answers grounded in real documents rather than relying on its training data.
The Complete RAG Pipeline


image.png


Here’s what happens when a user asks a question in a RAG-powered application:

Step 1: Embed the query.

The user’s question is converted to an embedding vector using the same model that embedded the documents. (Module 7 - sentence-transformers)

Step 2: Retrieve relevant documents.

The query vector is compared against all document vectors in the vector database. The top-K most similar chunks are returned. (Module 7 - ChromaDB)

Step 3: Build the prompt.

The retrieved chunks are combined with a system prompt and the user’s original question into a single prompt for the LLM. The system prompt instructs the model: "Answer based ONLY on the context provided."

Step 4: Generate the answer.

The LLM receives the complete prompt and generates a response grounded in the retrieved documents. (This module - Ollama or OpenAI API)

Step 5: Return the answer with citations.

The response is sent back to the user, ideally with references to which source documents the answer came from.

RAG vs. Fine-Tuning

Students often ask: "Why not just train the model on my documents?"

Fine-tuning (retraining a model on your data) is useful for changing the model’s style or teaching it a new skill. But it’s expensive, slow, and the model can still hallucinate because the knowledge is baked into weights rather than referenced from sources.

RAG is better for:

    Data that changes frequently (documentation, policies, news)
    Data that needs citations ("this answer came from page 47 of the manual")
    Privacy-sensitive data (documents stay in your database, never sent to a training pipeline)
    Rapid iteration (update documents, not retrain models)

The GenAI course you’ll take after this one covers fine-tuning in depth. For this course, RAG is the right tool.
